## n01

Дай определение парсека.

### 523d9c8ab0

Парсек (пк) — стандартная единица измерения расстояния в астрономии, определяемая следующим образом: представьте круг радиусом в одну астрономическую единицу (что равно среднему расстоянию от Земли до Солнца), на который мы смотрим перпендикулярно его плоскости. С расстояния ровно в один парсек радиус этого круга будет виден под углом в одну угловую секунду. Таким образом, 1 пк равен примерно 3,26 светового года и стольким же астрономическим единицам, сколько угловых секунд содержится в одном радиане: 206 264,8 астрономических единиц. Практическая важность этого определения заключается в его связи с методом определения расстояний по параллаксу. В этом методе измеряются крошечные смещения положения астрономических объектов на небе при смещении наблюдателя на заданное расстояние. Для астрономического объекта на расстоянии одного парсека смещение наблюдателя на одну астрономическую единицу (обычно из-за перемещения Земли по орбите между двумя наблюдениями) соответствует видимому угловому смещению положения в одну угловую секунду, называемому параллаксом объекта. Это приводит к простому соотношению: расстояние в парсеках равно единице, деленной на угол параллакса в угловых секундах. Непосредственный геометрический смысл парсека делает его предпочтительной единицей расстояния для профессиональных астрономов: в литературе расстояния чаще указывают в парсеках, чем в световых годах. Сириус находится на расстоянии 2,7 пк от нас, что соответствует параллаксу 1/2,7=0,37 угловых секунд. Даже Проксима Центавра, ближайшая звезда после нашего Солнца, имеет параллакс менее 1 угловой секунды.

## n02

Define occultation.

### 4ac1acf9a4

An occultation is when one object entirely hides another behind it. When viewed from Earth, the Moon frequently passes in front of stars. These stars are said to be occulted by the Moon. Other objects in the Solar System occasionally occult bright stars. Occultations are useful for studying the atmospheres of distant Kuiper Belt objects. When these icy bodies occult stars, the light from the star briefly passes through the Kuiper Belt object's atmosphere. This light is imprinted with the spectral signatures of the Kuiper Belt object's atmosphere when it reaches the Earth. When one object passes in front of another but does not entirely hide it, it is said to transit the other object.

## n03

Что такое приливный захват?

### 11f5d498e6

Приливная синхронизация — это процесс, при котором приливные силы изменяют вращение одного тела, обращающегося вокруг другого, меняя его период вращения. Хорошим примером этого является Луна. Под действием приливных сил, вызванных притяжением Земли, Луна слегка вытягивается, причём её длинная ось направлена к Земле. По мере обращения Луны вокруг Земли земное притяжение слабо воздействует на этот приливный выступ. Если бы вращение Луны отклонило её длинную ось от направления на Землю, приливная сила поворачивала бы Луну обратно, чтобы её приливный выступ снова был направлен к Земле. Это означает, что Луна всегда обращена к Земле одной и той же стороной, поскольку приливная синхронизация сделала её период вращения равным её орбитальному периоду. Такое соотношение один к одному между периодом вращения и периодом обращения выполняется не для всех тел, захваченных приливными силами. Сравнительно вытянутая эллиптическая орбита Меркурия вокруг Солнца в сочетании с приливным захватом приводит к тому, что Меркурий совершает три оборота вокруг своей оси за каждые два оборота вокруг Солнца.

## n04

What is a sidereal day?

### 926fb3dc20

In English as well as in numerous other languages, "day" has multiple meanings. "Day" in the sense of "daytime" describes the time when the Sun provides us with natural light, followed by the night when the Sun has set, and it is dark.  A day by this definition is shorter in the winter and longer in the summer in the northern hemisphere, while the opposite holds for the southern hemisphere. In the far north or the far south, there is a time of year where the Sun does not set at all – directly at the poles, the Sun is up for six months without setting! "Day" is also the name of the 24-hour unit of time we use in the calendar. Astronomically speaking, the time from one local noon to the next – that is, from the day's highest position of the Sun above the horizon from one day to the next – is called an apparent solar day. The length of local days varies depending on the time of year, owing to the facts that (a) Earth's orbit is elliptical (with Earth moving faster when it is closer to the Sun), and that (b) the apparent trajectory of the Sun in the sky is at different angles relative to Earth's equator. For practical purposes, timekeeping instead uses the average length of apparent solar days, which are called mean solar days. The associated time at 24 hours per day is called mean solar time. A sidereal day is based on the "fixed" background stars on the celestial sphere. It is the period between the time a "fixed" star reaches its highest point in the sky to the next time it reaches its highest point in the sky. One sidereal day lasts approximately 23 hours 56 minutes and 4 seconds. The discrepancy between this and an apparent solar day (24 hours) is caused by the Sun's apparent motion relative to the "fixed" background stars.

## n06

If the star gets closer, why does its measured parallax get larger? Use the glossary.

### 3912044ac6

Parallax is the apparent change in a celestial object's position on the sky caused by a change in the observer's viewpoint, measured against very distant reference objects.

The size of that apparent angular shift is inversely proportional to the object's distance from us.

So a closer star has a larger parallax angle than a more distant one, because the same observer shift (one astronomical unit, achieved by observing months apart) produces a bigger apparent displacement for the nearer object.

This relation is captured quantitatively by the parsec definition: distance in parsecs equals one divided by the parallax angle in arcseconds, consistent with the star at one parsec showing one arcsecond of parallax.

## n08

Compare a transit and an occultation by apparent angular size, and say whether viewing location can matter.

### 4d7cf8a343

In a transit, the closer body has a smaller apparent angular size than the body behind it, so it blocks only part of the surface. If the closer body has a larger angular size than the farther one, the event is called an occultation instead, since it can hide the farther body entirely.

Occultation means one object entirely hides another from the observer's viewpoint; when the front object only partly covers the other, it is said to transit.

Viewing location matters: a transit seen by one observer may not be visible to another observer viewing the same system from a different angle. A planet transits its star only when the orbit plane crosses the line of sight from the observer's position.

A concrete illustration: Phobos is too small in apparent size and wrongly placed to cover the whole solar disk as seen from Mars, so the event is a transit rather than a full covering. On Earth, the Moon can appear just the right size to cover the Sun.

### c5a40f95fa

A transit happens when a smaller-angular-size body passes between the observer and a larger-angular-size body, so it blocks only part of the obscured object's disk. When the obscuring body has a larger angular size than the object behind it and entirely hides it, the event is called an occultation rather than a transit.

An example of the size distinction: seen from Mars, the moon Phobos cannot completely cover the Sun's disk, so the event is a transit rather than an eclipse. On Earth, the Moon can appear large enough to cover the Sun completely.

Viewing location matters. A transit seen by one observer may not be visible to an observer viewing the same system from a different angle, and an exoplanet only transits its star when seen from a location whose line of sight intersects the orbital plane.

Because whether transits occur depends on orbital orientation relative to the observer, transit searches detect only a fraction of planets; a system can hold a planet without showing transits from our viewpoint.

## n10

Use “How astronomers detect exoplanets” to compare what transit depth and radial velocity tell you, and what combining them adds.

### 1bcf710751

Transit depth tells you the planet's size relative to its star: for a small opaque planet and a uniformly bright stellar disk, the fractional dip in brightness is roughly the square of the ratio of planet radius to stellar radius. Turning that into an actual planet radius requires knowing the star's radius.

Radial velocity measures the star's line-of-sight wobble through the Doppler shift of its spectral lines. With orbital and stellar information, this method typically yields only a minimum planet mass, since the orbital inclination is unknown.

Combining the two adds value: a transit supplies the orbital inclination, which helps convert the minimum mass from radial velocity into a true mass. Radius and mass together also give mean density, which constrains possible compositions.

The combination also increases confidence, since an eclipsing binary can imitate a transit and stellar activity can affect radial velocity measurements. Combining observations helps guard against these false positives.

### d67944c971

Transit depth measures the fractional dimming when a planet crosses the stellar disk; for a small opaque planet on a uniformly bright star, depth ≈ (planet radius / stellar radius)². So it gives a size estimate, but needs the star's radius to derive the planet's radius.

## n16

Use an article specifically about lunar tidal locking if there is one. If not, explain from the glossary why we keep seeing nearly the same side of the Moon.

### 4238db0b3e

Tidal locking is a process in which tidal forces alter the rotation of one body orbiting another, changing its rotation period. For the Moon, Earth's gravity stretches it subtly into an elongated shape with the long axis pointing toward Earth. If the Moon rotated so that bulge no longer faced Earth, the tidal force would drag it back.

Because of this, the Moon's rotation period has become equal to its orbital period, so it always presents the same face toward Earth. That is why we keep seeing nearly the same side of the Moon.

This one-to-one relationship is not universal for tidally locked bodies: Mercury rotates three times for every two orbits of the Sun because of its elliptical orbit combined with tidal locking.
